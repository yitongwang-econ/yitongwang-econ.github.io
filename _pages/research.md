## Working Papers

### Refund Frictions and the Transmission of Export VAT Rebates *(with Li Gan and Siyuan Zeng)*  
**Job Market Paper**  
[[Draft](/files/jmp.pdf)]

**Abstract:** How a tax incentive is paid can matter as much as its size. China raised export VAT rebate rates on 1,258 products in 2015. But firms received the increase in two ways, depending on their tax position: as an immediate cut in VAT owed, or as a refund paid on average 133 days later. Using the National Tax Survey Database and customs data in a triple-difference design, we find that refund-dependent firms respond less. They expand exports half as much, and their equipment response is indistinguishable from zero. A delayed refund costs the firm two things. Its present value is 98.2 percent of face value, and that discount explains only 3.9 percent of the export gap. It also comes too late to finance current production, since the firm holds the cash about half as many days as an immediate rebate. The rest of the gap is consistent with this working-capital friction. Firms that were financially tighter before the reform respond more strongly, but only where the rebate arrives immediately. A model calibrated to refund timing and balance sheets matches the estimated responses. Faster refunds and borrowing against pending claims each restore nearly the full response.

### Optimal Research Contracting with Observable Firm Characteristics
[[Draft](/files/research_contracting.pdf)]

**Abstract:** Government research funding programs face a fundamental challenge: firms' true research ability is private information. This paper shows that mechanisms based on observable firm characteristics (OFC) significantly improve the performance of NASA’s Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) program. Empirically, I find that patent intensity and past SBIR success rates are strong positive predictors of research ability, yet they receive minimal weight in the current contract. Moreover, the optimal OFC mechanism captures 61% of potential government payoff gains—defined as the gap between the pooling benchmark and the first-best—compared to only 5% under the current contract. These improvements arise primarily from reallocating budget resources away from high-ability firms, which would participate regardless, and toward lower-ability firms with higher participation elasticities.

### Should the Government Subsidize Firms or Households? *(with Li Gan and Yuzhe Zhang)*  
[Draft available upon request]

**Abstract:** This study focuses on the determinants of fiscal allocation between households and firms during the COVID-19 pandemic. Using data from 33 countries provided by the IMF, this paper finds that government budget, income inequality, and severity of COVID are all positively related to the ratio of household subsidies to firm subsidies. To theoretically explore these relationships, we develop a simple static model whose predictions align with our empirical findings. To capture the dynamic and nonlinear impacts of these factors, we then extend the model to a dynamic framework. This dynamic model allows us to quantify the extent to which these factors can explain the variation in fiscal allocation.

### The Optimal Inflation Rate *(with Shenghao Zhu)*  
[Draft available upon request]

**Abstract:** We derive an optimal inflation rate schedule in an infinite-horizon Aiyagari-Bewley-Huggett economy with idiosyncratic risk in labor income and investment income. First, we solve the Mean-Field Games with HJB and KFE equations and find that the wealth distribution in a special case with two income types has a generalized Pareto tail, along with a Dirac mass point at the lower boundary with low income. Second, the optimal inflation rate can be expressed in terms of sufficient statistics using a perturbation method. Third, an upwind scheme algorithm is designed to verify the theory and match empirical data. In an extension, we build a model with firms and an endogenous labor market to derive the relationship between the inflation rate, social welfare, and inequality.
